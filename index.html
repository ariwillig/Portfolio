Pacemaker Project

I designed and built a Bluetooth-enabled pacemaker from scratch to better understand the heart's electrical signals. The device integrates operational amplifier circuitry, signal filtering, C++ firmware, and a Bluetooth module into a single system that monitors cardiac activity and delivers a pacing pulse when the heart's own electrical activity fails. I iterated on the design by testing against ECG signals and frog heart tissue, eventually producing a fully functional pacemaker that induced heartbeats in response to simulated electrical failure. The pacemaker also transmitted measurements to my iPhone over Bluetooth, where past readings could be recorded and reviewed. The project gave me hands-on experience with analog circuit design, embedded programming, wireless communication, and in-vivo device testing.

Mercury Project

Methylmercury (MeHg) poisoning is a persistent global health threat. Past outbreaks have claimed hundreds of lives and left millions with lasting neurodevelopmental damage, and even sublethal exposure can cause neurological, cardiovascular, liver, and kidney injury. With a team of classmates, I developed a compartmental model of methylmercury mass flow, accumulation, and transformation in the human body, built from a system of differential equations representing the most vulnerable organs and systems. We simulated both healthy and unhealthy chronic exposure over a 60-day dosing period and compared how mercury accumulated in each organ in scope. The project strengthened my skills in physiological modeling, differential equations, and translating a real-world health problem into a quantitative framework.

Lightbox

This lightbox is a small but complete example of the medical device prototyping process, spanning mechanical design, electronics, and firmware. I modeled the enclosure in CAD (in the shape of Bert from Sesame Street), designed a custom PCB in KiCad, and soldered the components and an Arduino onto the board. I then wrote firmware, structured as a state machine, that controls three LEDs independently, with pulse duration and brightness adjustable through potentiometers mounted on the PCB. The project gave me hands-on experience with CAD, PCB design and assembly, embedded firmware, and integrating hardware and software into a working product.

Auralert Seizure Prediction Wearable

Auralert is a non-invasive, wearable system that predicts tonic-clonic seizures for people with drug-resistant epilepsy, giving them time to move to a safe space. Along with a four-person team, I took the project from problem definition through a working, integrated prototype, following a full design control process: stakeholder interviews with clinicians and patients, gap analysis, idea screening and scoring, proof-of-principle testing, FMEA, and regulatory and ethics reviews. The system combines a behind-the-ear 3-lead EEG electrode holder, a custom PCB in a back-mounted processing unit with an adjustable fabric harness, and a dual-input machine learning model (a CNN on raw EEG plus an MLP on bandpower features). The model is hosted on a web server and classifies a user's state as interictal, pre-ictal, or ictal. Classifications are sent to an iPhone app that alerts the user when a seizure is imminent or underway.

Across the project we designed, built, and tested every layer of this pipeline. On the hardware side, we built an analog front-end to capture EEG signals and validated it against a laboratory-grade iWorx EEG system, with matching spectral content. We also iterated through 3D-printed and cast behind-the-ear enclosures and a custom PCB. On the software side, we designed a dual-input classifier which reached 0.92 accuracy and a 0.959 ROC-AUC on held-out CHB-MIT clinical EEG data (when detecting seizures). A Wi-Fi-enabled microcontroller transmits EEG data to a FastAPI-hosted model, and classifications are delivered to an iPhone app that alerts the user. In full-pipeline testing on non-epileptic subjects, the wearable classified interictal states at nearly 100% while stationary or moving the head. Walking introduced motion artifacts that degraded performance, which we identified as the main target for future work along with clinical trials on epileptic patients and larger training datasets. The project gave me hands-on experience with biopotential circuit design, embedded systems, ML for biosignals, rapid prototyping, and design control documentation.
